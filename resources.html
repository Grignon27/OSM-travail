Theory is an essential part of your child's musical journey, and these resources are here to help with theory work at home.

The Theory Help Sheet carries a number of important reminders for understanding theory and getting through homework. Theory is the grammar of music, and it is a very visual subject — especially while a student is still meeting all the concepts for the first time.

Our Flute and Theory teacher also runs a YouTube channel called Theory in a Nutshell, where short videos cover individual theory and harmony concepts, gathered into playlists by RCM level. For younger students there are fun pages to try as well, including matching and word games.

This week Our first hidden treasure

Joseph Bologne, Chevalier de Saint-Georges

Born on 25 December 1745 in Guadeloupe, then a French colony, Joseph Bologne was the son of a wealthy plantation owner and a young enslaved woman. He was sent to Paris to be educated at the age of seven, and two years later his mother, Nanon, came to Paris as well.

He was not only a great musician — playing the violin, composing and conducting — but also a wonderful dancer and an outstanding fencer. He almost certainly met Mozart, and he certainly met Haydn. He composed string quartets, violin concertos, two symphonies, sonatas, operas and vocal music, and his work has become far better known in recent years, helped by the film Chevalier, made about his life. He died in Paris in 1799 after a long illness.

The composers, the instruments and the stories behind the music your child is learning.

One thing music teachers get asked over and over is how to get a child to practise more. There is no easy answer — practice tends to wax and wane naturally, and it changes at different ages. What doesn't change is that learning an instrument needs consistent practice.

Specific goals matter. So does regularity: one two-hour session a week is far less productive than four thirty-minute ones, and your child's teacher can set a sensible weekly goal. As a child gets older and more advanced the sessions lengthen, but length always matters less than content — going over each piece mindlessly until the timer runs out is not a productive practice, and a shorter, focused session can achieve more.

When life gets busy, some practice beats none. Try to set practice times that are generally easy to keep, and avoid squeezing one in when your child is exhausted or about to leave for a birthday party. The Parent Handbook has plenty more tips as well.
