Bethany Goluboff

Violin

Bethany has traveled a circuitous route from California to Finland to Québec to Ontario. Born into a musical family with professional musician parents, Bethany has been playing the violin since the age of four, starting with the Suzuki method. She studied with Manuel Compinsky, renowned violinist of the Compinsky Trio, Toscanini's NBC Orchestra, and master teacher of many prominent violinists. She studied at the Sibelius Academy in Helsinki for two years before returning to North America and pursued studies at Laval University and the Conservatory of Music in Québec City. Bethany holds a B.M. and M.M. in Violin Performance in addition to a B.A. in Music Education, all from Laval University in Quebec City. She performed with the Quebec Symphony Orchestra for two years. Before coming to Oakville, she spent three years in Sudbury as Concert Master of the Sudbury Symphony, member of the Sudbury Symphony String Quartet, and the principal teacher with the Sudbury Symphony School of Music. Bethany has completed Suzuki teacher training for Books 1 through 10. Her mentors included Alice Joy Lewis, Pat d'Ercole and Ed Sprunger. In Oakville, Bethany has performed annually with various orchestras and groups, including The Oakville Chamber Orchestra and Masterworks of Oakville, for the past two decades. She taught violin for the Burlington Suzuki Association from 2004-2009 and the Oakville Suzuki Association from 2008 onward. Bethany's students have won RCM Gold Awards, as well as many first and second places and scholarships in music festival competitions. She loves teaching and watching her students develop a love of music as well as the many personal skills and self-confidence they acquire when studying the violin.

What Is Suzuki?

The Suzuki method, developed by Shinichi Suzuki, emphasizing learning music in a nurturing environment and teaching the “whole” child.

Dr.Suzuki
Jean Grieve

This approach to music pedagogy is based on the belief that every child can learn to play an instrument when provided with proper encouragement and a nurturing environment. Suzuki believed that learning an instrument was akin to learning a language. Using this “Mother tongue” approach, whereby children learn to speak by listening to the language spoken to, and around them, students listen to their music as well as learn by ear to begin.

Listening to music is an essential part of the learning process as is parental involvement, practice and repetition, learning in small steps, review and group classes.

Group classes are important as they foster community, problem solving, cooperation, self esteem, ensemble playing, leadership skills and reinforce techniques learned in private lessons.

Jean Grieve

Jean Grieve has been the driving force behind the Suzuki method in Oakville for decades. Her warmth and dedication with students has inspired several generations of musicians.

Our Flute teacher, Michelle Coon, was able to sit down with Jean as she shared some memories and her thoughts on the Suzuki methodology. Here are some excerpts.

Suzuki & RCM

How does Suzuki fit with the Royal Conservatory of Music (RCM)?

Perfectly well!

There is a sense out there that at some point it's time to "stop Suzuki and go to RCM," which is a misunderstanding of what each thing is. Suzuki is a methodology for teaching music and the RCM is a Certificate Program. They are not mutually exclusive.

Students can benefit from exams for various reasons. Some students only do one or two exams during their journey, others do more, and for some students exams are not helpful for them — it really depends on the student. RCM practical exams have several components including repertoire, etudes/studies, scales, ear training and sight reading.

From Level 5 RCM onwards, there is a Theory Rudiments co-requisite, and from Level 9 there are two co-requisites, Harmony and History. These co-requisites are necessary to obtain the official RCM Certificate. Students can receive external High School Credits through RCM exams — the RCM worked with the Provincial governments to secure this designation. Level 7 (practical and theory) equals a Grade 11 credit and Level 8 (practical and theory) equals a Grade 12 external credit. The Practical exam grade and Theory exam grade are weighted equally in the determination of the final grade.

Many pieces in the Suzuki Books are found in the RCM Syllabi.

Appendix A: A Comparison

The Suzuki Method

  • A methodology and philosophy, including the Suzuki triangle of parent, teacher and student
  • Progressive repertoire — building blocks, step-by-step sequential work
  • Teachers must have Suzuki teacher training, are encouraged to continue professional development, and must be a member in good standing with the SAA
  • Group lessons are essential
  • Serves beginner to advanced students
  • Has a series of books based on a step-by-step progression of development
  • No official certification program
  • Has a central association (the SAA) with branches all over the world, running annual institutes taught by certified teacher trainers
  • Taught as a methodology in post-secondary institutions — a Master's degree in Suzuki pedagogy is possible

Royal Conservatory of Music (RCM)

  • No specific methodology
  • Progressive repertoire in groupings of skill level
  • Anyone can teach with an RCM exam as the goal — no equivalent teacher-training requirement
  • Group lessons are not applicable
  • Serves beginner to advanced students
  • Has a series of level books grouped by era-based listings
  • Main role is certification, including external high school credits
  • Has a central location (Toronto) and services all of Canada and the US
  • Has an "RCM Certified Teacher" designation based on number of students sent to exams and their marks

Group Classes

Group classes are a cornerstone of Suzuki teaching. They foster community with the children all working towards a common goal. Children learn from each other; they watch the others in the group as well as the teacher and it can help them overcome obstacles.

  • They feel less vulnerable when they realize “Johnny” has the same difficulty or “Janice” can’t play the new song all the way through yet either. They are not alone.
  • They learn new strategies, new repertoire and make friends. All of a sudden “working” on a piece becomes less like work and more like fun.
  • They develop performance skills and musicianship.
  • They develop ensemble skills and how to work with others.
  • They develop discipline and concentration skills.

Humans are social beings and we need to foster safe and real places for non-screen interaction and proper, respectful social skills.
